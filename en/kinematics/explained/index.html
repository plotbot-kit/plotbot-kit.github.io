Understanding inverse kinematics: how PlotBot finds its motor angles

PlotBot has two motors, and each can do just one thing: turn its upper arm to an angle. A drawing, though, is made of points on paper, such as “30 mm to the right, 100 mm forward”. So for every point the robot has to find the two motor angles that put the pen exactly there.

That problem is called inverse kinematics. “Inverse” because you work backwards, from the pen to the motors. You can solve it with a compass, a ruler and a protractor. This page walks through it in seven steps, for kids from about 12 and their parents.

The arm is simple. The two motor shafts sit 36 mm apart. Each carries a 65 mm upper arm with a 90 mm forearm attached. The joint between them is called the elbow here. The forearms meet in another joint, and the pen sits right on that joint's axis.

The baseline is the dashed line through both motors. A motor angle is 0° when the upper arm points right along it. From there it grows anticlockwise: 90° points straight ahead, 180° points left.

Try it yourself

Three tasks for the figure above. Solve one and a tick appears next to it.

  1. Find a point where the left motor is at 90°.
    What do you notice?

    The left upper arm then points straight ahead. There isn't just one such point but a whole arc of them: the elbow stays put, and the pen travels around it on a circle with a 90 mm radius.

  2. Put the pen exactly above the midpoint between the motors (x = 0). Add up the two motor angles.
    Answer

    The sum is always 180°, however far forward the pen is. On the centre line the arms mirror each other: if the left upper arm is at 155°, for example, the right one is at 25°.

  3. How far forward can the pen go? Move it up until it's just still reachable.
    Answer

    At x = 0 it stops just short of y = 154 mm. By then the upper arm and forearm lie almost in one line, 65 + 90 = 155 mm in total. Because the motors sit 18 mm off-centre, the pen falls about a millimetre short of 155 mm.

Seven steps with compass, ruler and protractor

The example: the pen should go to the point (30, 100), which is 30 mm right of centre and 100 mm in front of the motors.

  1. Draw circles around the motors

    Set the compass to 65 mm, the length of an upper arm, and draw a circle around each motor. The elbow sits at the end of the upper arm. Wherever the motor turns, the elbow stays on this circle.

  2. Mark the target point

    Mark the point where the pen should end up. In the example that's (30, 100). The dashed square is PlotBot's drawing area.

  3. Draw a circle around the target

    Set the compass to 90 mm, the length of a forearm, and draw a circle around the target point. The forearm joins the elbow to the pen. So each elbow is 90 mm from the pen and sits on this circle.

  4. Look at the crossing points

    The circle around the target crosses each motor circle twice. Only at these four points do both distances fit: 65 mm to the motor and 90 mm to the pen. So each motor has two possible elbows: two points on the blue circle and two on the orange one.

  5. Pick the right elbow

    PlotBot uses the outer crossing point on each motor circle: the left one on the left motor's blue circle, the right one on the right motor's orange circle. That keeps each arm on its own side. With the other two points the upper arms would reach across the other motor; the picture shows those arms faintly. Over about two thirds of the drawing area the arms would then cross and collide.

  6. Draw the arms

    Use a ruler to join each motor to its elbow: those are the upper arms. Then join each elbow to the target: those are the forearms. This is the position of the arms when the pen is at the target.

  7. Read off the motor angles

    Place the protractor on each motor with zero pointing right. Read off where the upper arm points. In the example that's 119° on the left and 22° on the right. When the robot turns its motors to these two angles, the pen ends up at (30, 100).

For the curious

Checking it with the law of cosines

You can also calculate the motor angles without drawing anything. The tool for that is the law of cosines, which you usually meet at school around age 15. Motor, elbow and pen form a triangle whose three sides are known: the 65 mm upper arm, the 90 mm forearm and the distance c from the motor to the pen. For the angle α at the motor:

cos α = (65² + c² − 90²) / (2 · 65 · c)

In the example the pen is 48 mm to the right of the left motor and 100 mm in front of it. Pythagoras gives c = 110.9 mm, and the law of cosines gives α = 54.2°. The line from the motor to the pen rises at φ = 64.4°. The upper arm lies α further round to the left: 64.4° + 54.2° = 118.6°.

The right side is the mirror image, so there α is subtracted: c = 100.7 mm, φ = 83.2°, α = 61.4°, which makes 83.2° − 61.4° = 21.8°. Rounded, those are the 119° and 22° the protractor shows.

The robot itself calculates differently but arrives at the same angles. Its firmware does the compass construction with numbers: from the coordinates it works out where the circle around the motor and the circle around the pen cross. Then it takes the outer crossing point and finds the direction from the motor to that point. That's the motor angle.

Where the pen can go, and where it can't

The construction doesn't work for every point. If the target is more than 155 mm from a motor, the arms are too short: upper arm and forearm together measure 65 + 90 = 155 mm. The circle around the target then doesn't reach the motor circle, and there is no elbow.

Very close to a motor it fails too. The forearm is 25 mm longer than the upper arm, so even folded up completely the pen stays 25 mm away from the motor shaft.

The robot adds limits of its own. The left upper arm swings between 78° and 238°, the right one between −58° and 102°. Green is the area that's left. For drawing, PlotBot uses the dashed 80 × 80 mm square inside it. There the arms are neither almost straight nor tightly folded. In those positions even a small error at the motor would shift the pen a long way.
